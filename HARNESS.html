01

What I meant by standoffs

A standoff is just a spacer that holds a board off whatever is behind it — normally a threaded pillar you bolt through the PCB’s mounting holes. I used the word loosely, and it’s worth being precise now that you’re building: the AI-Thinker ESP32-CAM has no mounting holes. Look at images/esp32_back.jpeg — it’s a bare rectangle with headers down both long edges and nothing to screw into.

So in this housing the standoff is a printed feature: two ledges moulded into the shell that the board’s long edges sit on, with a clip or the closing lid holding it down. The job is the same as a screw standoff — keep the solder side off the plastic, leave room for the dupont shells, let air move — it’s just done with geometry instead of hardware.

02

Should the breadboard live in the housing?

Yes, with one rule and one plan.

The rule: the battery never touches the breadboard. Four 18650s in parallel will pour a hundred-plus amps into a short, and a spring contact is exactly the wrong thing to have in that path. Battery goes to the module’s VIN pads directly, through a fuse and a polarized plug, and stops there. Only the regulated 3.3 V side ever reaches the breadboard.

The plan: treat it as a layout you will later transfer. A solderless contact is a spring and a bit of oxide, and the failure mode it produces — a momentary open during a 300 mA WiFi burst — looks exactly like the brownout symptoms you’ve already been chasing: camera init failures and SD corruption, intermittent, in a unit bolted to a wall. Indoors, with no vibration and no thermal cycling, it will probably be fine for a long time. But when the layout below is proven, move it onto a breadboard-pattern proto PCB — a permanent board with the identical five-hole column pattern. The layout transfers hole-for-hole with no redesign, and the contacts become solder.

That also settles the prototyping worry. Solid-core wire ends plug into a breadboard and solder into a proto board. Nothing you do today closes a door.

04

The buck–boost: which hole, which wire

Strip 3 mm, twist, tin the wire, tin the pad, then reflow the two together at about 350 °C with flux. Bring all four wires out of whichever face will not sit against the printed ledges. Once you’ve tested it, a blob of hot glue over the four wire entries is your strain relief — those pads lift if a wire gets tugged.

Before the iron touches anything

Connect the pack to VIN/GND with nothing else attached and measure OUT→GND. That tells you what’s actually bridged. I can see a solder blob at the 5V position in your photo, which matches what you assumed, but the image isn’t sharp enough for me to call it — and the meter settles it in five seconds.

05

The breadboard map

A 170-point mini board is 17 columns of 5 holes on each side of the channel, and no dedicated power rails — so a “rail” here is a column, and it only has five holes. That’s not enough for ground, so column 1 is bridged across the channel with a single jumper (E1–G1), turning both halves into one 8-hole node. Column 3 gets the same treatment for 3V3.

Everything else follows from keeping the two long runs out of each other’s way: the pull-up lives in row B, the emitter jumper in row D, the base resistor in row E. No two spans share a row.

Transistor orientation

Insert the TO-92 into C8/C9/C10 with its flat face pointing toward the centre channel. The middle leg is the base on every part in this family, so C9 is right either way. In that orientation a 2N3904 reads E–B–C left to right, which is what the map shows. A BC547/BC548 is the mirror image — if that’s what you have, swap column 8 and column 10: the jumper goes to C10’s column and the pull-up plus GPIO 13 wire go to C8’s.

You don’t have to trust either of us. Step 6 below measures it.

06

Why the inverter is there at all

The AM312 idles LOW and pulses HIGH for ~2 s on motion. Wired straight to GPIO 13 that would be fine electrically — and fatal to the SD card, because GPIO 13 is D3 and a card that samples D3 low during init comes up in SPI mode. So the transistor flips it:

  • Idle. PIR output low → no base current → transistor off → the 10 k pulls the collector to 3.3 V. GPIO 13 sits HIGH, which is exactly what the card wants, and no current flows through the pull-up. Sleep cost: zero.
  • Motion. PIR drives 3.3 V → (3.3−0.7)/10 k ≈ 260 µA of base current → transistor saturates → collector pulled to ~0.1 V → ext0 fires on the falling edge. The pull-up burns 330 µA for those two seconds. Irrelevant.
  • Sensor unplugged. Base floats at 0 V through nothing, transistor stays off, pin stays HIGH — reads as “no motion”. The firmware’s INPUT_PULLUP agrees. Fails safe.

Your external 10 k also dominates the ESP32’s internal ~45 k pull-up, which gives a cleaner, faster falling edge into the wake comparator than the internal one alone.

07

Bring-up order

Do not skip ahead. Each step is cheap; the failure each one catches is not.

  1. Measure what the module does now

    Pack → VIN/GND, nothing else. Read OUT→GND. Write it down. This is your evidence that the jumper move actually did something.

  2. Move the jumper

    Flux the existing blob, add a little fresh solder to it — counterintuitive, but it makes the old joint flow — then wick it off. Confirm the 5V pair no longer beeps, and neither does 4V2. Then flux the 3V3 pads and drag a small ball across. Confirm only the 3V3 pair beeps.

  3. Verify 3.30 V, loaded

    Power VIN again: OUT must read 3.25–3.35 V. Then hang ~10 Ω across it (≈330 mA, the ESP32’s TX peak) for a few seconds and confirm it holds. A converter that sags here will brown out the camera later.

  4. Solder the four wires

    Stranded to VIN/GND with the fuse and polarized plug on the far end; solid core to OUT/GND. Re-check OUT at the far end of the solid-core wires before going further.

  5. Build the breadboard — no ESP32, no PIR

    Nets, bridges, both capacitors (watch the electrolytic’s stripe → column 1), transistor, both resistors, jumper. Power it up and probe the spare holes: B1/J1 must be 0 V, D3/J3 must be 3.3 V. Then probe the collector column — it should read ~3.3 V.

  6. Add the PIR and test the inverter

    Three wires to I3/I1/A6. Meter on the collector column. Idle: ~3.3 V. Wave your hand: it must drop below 0.3 V for about two seconds, then return. If it does the opposite, your transistor is a BC547-style pinout — swap columns 8 and 10. This is the test that makes the orientation question moot.

  7. Only now plug in the ESP32-CAM

    3V3, GND, GPIO 13. Nothing goes to the 5V pin. If step 3 or step 6 hasn’t passed, this step can cost you a board.

  8. Watch a full cycle on serial

    Motion → wake → SD mount → 300 frames → upload → sleep, several times running. Then pull the ESP32’s 3V3 dupont, put the meter in series with it, and get the deep-sleep number — that’s the measurement the whole cell-count decision is waiting on.
